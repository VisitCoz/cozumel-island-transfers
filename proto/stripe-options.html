Checkout vs Payment Element — and the USD answer

Both options charge the same money and both take US cards. The difference is whose page the card is typed on, and what that costs you in compliance and trust.

Answer to your main issue first: charging in USD is not the problem, and it has nothing to do with this choice. A Mexican Stripe account can charge in over 135 currencies — USD included. What Mexico cannot do is get paid out in USD. Scroll to the money section; it's worth more than the rest of this page.

Why it wins for your buyer. The address bar says stripe.com at the exact moment she is most nervous about a Mexican website. She may not know Stripe, but the browser padlock and a domain that isn't yours reads as "a real payment company is handling this."

Why it wins for you. The card never touches your page, so you sit in the lightest PCI bracket (SAQ A) — a short self-assessment, not an audit. Apple Pay and Google Pay come free, which for a 60-something with an iPhone is one thumbprint instead of typing sixteen digits. Stripe handles 3-D Secure, retries, wallets, and every new card rule without you touching the site again.

The cost: the guest leaves your site for one screen. You can put your logo and colours on it, but it is Stripe's page, not yours.

Why you might want it. One unbroken flow, your design end to end, and the total stays visible next to the card fields. On brand, it is the nicer of the two.

What it costs. The card fields are on your page, so you move up to SAQ A-EP — a much longer self-assessment, and you become responsible for the security of every script that page loads. You also build and maintain the error handling, 3-D Secure step-up, and wallet buttons yourself. Stripe still hosts the fields in an iframe, so card data doesn't literally land on your server — but the compliance scope follows the page, not the iframe.

The honest summary: more work, more liability, and for a buyer who is scared of being scammed abroad, it removes the one reassurance she'd actually recognise.

The USD question — the real one

Charging USD is easy. Getting paid in USD is where Mexico bites.

  • Presentment currency — what the guest is charged. A Mexican Stripe account can charge in 135+ currencies including USD. Your prices stay in dollars, her statement says dollars. Solved.
  • Settlement currency — what lands in your bank. Stripe supports multi-currency payouts in AE, AU, CH, EU, GB, HK, LI, NO, SG and US. Mexico is not on that list. A Stripe MX account pays out in pesos, and Stripe converts every dollar you charge — taking 2% to do it.

At 300 bookings a year that's about $4,500 — every year — for nothing but the flag on the account. And it lands on top of the fact that your guests are all on international cards, which is the one surcharge you can never avoid from Mexico.

This is the same problem already in your notes — the $34.80 wire fee on USD receipts from TUI and Celebrity, and the US LLC idea you parked to solve it. It's the same fix, and Stripe roughly doubles the reason to do it.

What I'd verify before deciding, because I can't see your account: open Stripe → Settings → Payouts and check what currencies it offers you. The documentation says Mexico is MXN-only, but your account's actual options are the fact that matters. If it does offer USD, attach the Bancomer USD account (C4) and the whole table above collapses.

What I'd do

Take Checkout. Less code than you have now, lighter compliance, Apple Pay for free, and the address bar says stripe.com at the moment your buyer is deciding whether to trust a Mexican website. The only thing you give up is one screen of your own design.

Charge in USD from day one — that part works today and needs no decision.

Then treat the 2% conversion plus the peso payout as a separate, bigger question. It's a Knox and Fiscal conversation, not a website one, and it's worth about $15 a booking. Don't let it hold up launch — but don't let it quietly run for two years either.
